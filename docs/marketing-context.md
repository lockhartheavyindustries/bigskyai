## Target Audience

These are working strategy hypotheses, not verified customer research:

**Target companies and groups:** Small businesses, families, private
communities, and owners of specialized workflows. This is a working scope, not
a qualified-account list.

**Decision-makers:** Owners, operators, family organizers, and product or
technology leaders. Their exact buying roles have not been validated.

**Primary use case:** Identify a narrow workflow where a private, governed AI
system can deliver practical value and be responsibly operated over time.

1. Montana small-business owners who see practical AI opportunities but need a
   trustworthy builder and operator.
2. Families or small private communities that want useful AI in familiar
   communication channels without adopting another app.
3. Owners of high-consequence workflows who value evidence, privacy,
   governance, and ongoing care.
4. Product and technology leaders evaluating concrete examples of applied AI.

**Jobs to be done:**

- Understand what a practical, operated AI system looks like beyond a demo.
- Evaluate whether a private workflow could benefit from a scoped AI build.
- See credible implementation evidence, limitations, and operating discipline.
- Start a conversation without being pushed into a generic sales funnel.

No customer interviews, funnel data, or validated personas are recorded in
this repository. Replace hypotheses with approved evidence when it exists.

## Personas

These roles are hypotheses only:

- **Owner/operator and decision maker:** Needs a credible view of scope, risk,
  ongoing care, and what will change in daily work.
- **Workflow champion:** Understands the recurring problem and can explain the
  current process, exceptions, and desired outcome.
- **Participant or user:** Needs the system to fit familiar tools, respect
  privacy, and remain understandable.

## Problems & Pain Points

**Core problem:** People can see potential in AI but often lack a safe,
specific path from a general-purpose demo to a useful system they can trust.

**Why common alternatives may fall short:** Generic assistants may not know the
workflow, preserve evidence, fit existing channels, or include ongoing
operational care. This is strategic framing, not comparative research.

**What it costs:** Time spent manually gathering fragmented information,
repeating context, checking unsupported output, and maintaining brittle
one-off automations. No approved dollar, time-savings, or productivity metric
is available.

**Emotional tension:** Curiosity about what AI could do exists alongside
anxiety about privacy, reliability, loss of control, and hype.

## Competitive Landscape

No formal competitor study is recorded here.

- **Direct alternatives:** Other consultants or builders offering custom AI
  workflow implementation and ongoing operation.
- **Secondary alternatives:** General-purpose AI subscriptions, packaged
  automation tools, and internal experimentation.
- **Indirect alternatives:** Keep the workflow manual, postpone the decision,
  or stop after a prototype.

Do not claim these alternatives perform poorly without current evidence.

## Differentiation

**Core promise:** Practical AI systems that are built for real work and show
their evidence.

**Supporting ideas:**

- Systems are installed, governed, documented, and cared for—not merely demoed.
- Private data and public proof are kept deliberately separate.
- The work meets people in tools and routines they already use.
- Small, specific systems can create more value than a broad generic chatbot.
- Big Sky AI is rooted in Bozeman, Montana, while the work is not limited to a
  single local industry.

**Key differentiators:** Public implementation evidence, explicit privacy and
governance boundaries, integration with familiar routines, and ongoing system
care.

**Built differently:** The work is framed as an installed and governed system,
not a one-time prompt demo. Claims, operating state, and limitations are kept
distinct.

**Why customers choose—or may choose—Big Sky AI:** They may value a senior
hands-on builder, concrete examples, restrained scope, and visible evidence.
This is a positioning hypothesis, not verbatim customer research.

**Strategic distinction:** The brand should feel experienced, candid,
technically credible, humane, and lightly playful. Avoid both breathless AI
hype and defensive anti-AI rhetoric.

Do not publish superiority claims about competitors, providers, models, or
methods without current evidence.

## Objections and Anti-Personas

**Likely objection:** “AI output is unreliable or impossible to audit.”
**Response:** Show the evidence gates, source distinctions, human review, and
limitations that the specific public installation actually documents.

**Likely objection:** “A custom system will become another brittle tool to
maintain.” **Response:** Explain the scoped build and ongoing-care model without
promising uptime, response time, or support terms that are not published.

**Likely objection:** “Private information will become marketing material.”
**Response:** Point to the repository's explicit public/private boundary and
use only approved, anonymized examples.

**Anti-persona:** A buyer seeking unsupported autonomy claims, aggressive
growth promises, opaque surveillance, or an instant commodity app is not the
intended fit.

## Switching Dynamics

- **Push:** Repetitive manual work, fragmented information, and generic AI that
  forgets workflow context.
- **Pull:** A narrow, documented system that works in familiar channels and
  exposes its evidence.
- **Habit:** Existing manual processes are understood, controllable, and may be
  safer than an unclear replacement.
- **Anxiety:** Privacy, reliability, maintenance, vendor dependence, and loss of
  human judgment.

These forces are positioning hypotheses, not customer-study findings.

## Brand Voice

**Tone:** Clear, confident, practical, curious, warm, and occasionally dry.

**Personality:** Experienced, candid, technically credible, humane, and lightly
playful.

**Style:** Plain English with concrete examples. Lead with the human problem
and observable result, then explain enough of the system to make the proof
legible. Humor can make the work approachable but must not trivialize privacy,
security, financial risk, or the people being served.

**Voice do:**

- Use specific project names and verified examples.
- Explain technical controls in language a non-specialist can understand.
- Distinguish installations, concepts, publications, and experiments.
- State limitations and uncertainty as part of the evidence.
- Use “Big Sky AI,” “Bozeman, Montana,” “practical AI,” and “AI that shows its
  work” where naturally relevant.
- Keep calls to action conversational: explore the work, read the evidence, or
  start a conversation at the published contact address.

**Voice do not:**

- Use generic claims such as “revolutionary,” “game-changing,” “autonomous,” or
  “enterprise-grade” without concrete proof.
- Add fabricated client names, testimonials, savings, returns, usage metrics,
  partnerships, endorsements, availability, or delivery timelines.
- Turn a product concept into a shipped product or an installation into a
  generally available service.
- Imply that illustrative or reconstructed material is live private data.
- Use fear, manufactured urgency, or promises that remove human judgment from
  consequential decisions.
